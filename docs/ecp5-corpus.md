# ECP5 real-world bitstream corpus

Generated by `scripts/ecp5_corpus_report.py`. **Do not hand-edit.**

## Why this corpus exists

The ECP5 lifter was verified against 18 designs, and every one of them
was built by our own yosys + nextpnr + prjtrellis flow, with nextpnr's
placed netlist as the oracle. That is a closed loop: the same project
produced the bitstream, the reference netlist *and* the tile database,
so it cannot detect an assumption shared across all three.

This corpus opens the loop. It is bitstreams **other people built**,
plus bitstreams **Lattice Diamond built** — a wholly independent
implementation of the same silicon.

## The three claims, kept apart

These are routinely conflated, and conflating them overstates what a
decoder has been shown to do. Reported separately throughout:

| Claim | Meaning | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **decode** | parsed to a `.config`, CRC verified, all frames consumed | weak on its own — a wrong geometry can still 'succeed' |
| **oracle** | that `.config` is line-identical to `ecpunpack` | strong — an independent implementation, works on any ECP5 file |
| **lift** | netlist recovered and self-consistent | weakest — without source, only consistency is checkable, not exactness |

`ecpunpack` is the load-bearing oracle. Without a source netlist there
is nothing to check the *lift* against for exactness, so 'lifted' means
'structurally sane', never 'correct'.

**Coverage differs by claim.** Decode and oracle were run over all
**243** decoded files; the lift stage costs minutes per 85F
design and has been run over **92** of them so far. Gap rates
below are therefore proportions *of the lifted subset*, and the
subset is not a random sample — it is roughly corpus order. Treat
the decode/oracle counts as complete and the lift rates as
indicative.

## Device identification

The part is read from the bitstream's `VERIFY_ID` record and matched
against `devices.json`, **not** taken from the filename. Filenames in
this corpus are frequently wrong or absent, and decoding an 85F as a
12F does not fail loudly — it silently produces a plausible, wrong
fabric. The IDCODE is scanned before geometry is chosen.

## Which known gaps do real designs actually hit?

The lifter has three documented gaps. All three **under-connect**
rather than mis-connect, which is the safe direction: a missing
edge is visible as an unconnected pin, a wrong edge is not. How
hard real designs lean on them:

- **CCU2 carry path** (FCI/FCO unmodelled): exercised by **85/92** lifted designs
- **Wide muxes** (F5MUX/PFUMX/L6MUX21): exercised by **233/243** *decoded* designs (88/92 of the lifted subset), totalling **538,196** arcs into F5x/FXx pins. This is the most widely
  hit gap in the corpus by a wide margin — near-universal in real
  designs, and barely present in the 18 designs the lifter was
  originally verified against.
- **Clock globals** (prjtrellis parks non-TAP/SPINE globals at (0,0), so the lifter drops them): **43/92** designs have at least one FF whose clock resolved to a constant

  Share of FFs losing their clock, across the 43 affected
  designs: min 9.9%, **median 42.8%**, max 68.6%.

  **This is the headline result of the corpus.** Our own test
  designs lose 0% — including the Diamond builds, which is why
  building more of them would never have surfaced this. Real
  designs lose roughly half their flip-flop clocks.

  Root cause, confirmed by inspecting the decoded `.config`: the
  globals that get dropped are `G_DCS*` (dynamic clock select),
  `G_HPFE*`, and PLL outputs (`G_JLLCPLL*CLKO*`). Our test
  designs drive registers straight from a pad, so every global
  they use is `G_HPBX`/`G_VPTX` — the class prjtrellis *does*
  position. Any design with a PLL, which is essentially every
  real one, routes its main clock through the class it does not.

  Worst-affected designs:

  - `emard_ulx3s-bin__ulx3s_85f_cache_test.bit.a26cdb5f.…` — 4434/6468 FFs (68.6%)
  - `emard_ulx3s-bin__ulx3s_85f_oberon.bit.7c7f648f.gz` — 5824/10934 FFs (53.3%)
  - `dok3r_ulx3s-saxonsoc__saxonsoc-ulx3s-linux-12.1d7a6…` — 11822/22726 FFs (52.0%)
  - `emard_ulx3s-bin__ulx3s_12f_eth_hex.5bcf94cc.bit` — 2138/4286 FFs (49.9%)
  - `emard_ulx3s-bin__ulx3s_85f_eth_hex.de0776c8.bit` — 2116/4286 FFs (49.4%)
  - `dok3r_ulx3s-saxonsoc__saxonsoc-ulx3s-linux-12.f8a09…` — 8672/19854 FFs (43.7%)

Note the direction of every one of these. A dropped clock leaves a
flip-flop with `clk=1'b0` — visibly wrong, and safe. It never
silently attaches the register to the *wrong* clock, which is what
unioning through a (0,0)-parked global would do. Across the whole
corpus the fused-net count is the number to watch, and it is zero.
